1. Nucleic acids: information molecules

DNA and RNA store, transmit, and help express hereditary information. DNA is mainly the long-term information archive, while RNA helps transfer, regulate, and use that information during gene expression and protein synthesis.

Remember: DNA → RNA → protein describes the usual flow of sequence information during gene expression.
Check your understanding: Which statement best explains why RNA is essential even though DNA stores genetic information?

2. Build a nucleotide

Every nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a 5-carbon sugar, and a phosphate group. DNA uses deoxyribose; RNA uses ribose.

Common mistake: the nitrogenous base is attached to the sugar; it is not part of the repeating sugar–phosphate backbone.

3. Phosphodiester bonds and 5′→3′ directionality

A phosphodiester bond is a strong covalent linkage connecting the 3′ carbon/3′ OH of one sugar to the 5′ phosphate of the next nucleotide. Repeating linkages form the sugar–phosphate backbone.

During nucleic-acid polymerization, the incoming nucleotide arrives as a nucleotide triphosphate. Its addition occurs at the 3′ OH of the growing strand, so the new strand extends in the 5′→3′ direction.

AP tip: If asked why synthesis has directionality, connect the answer to the chemical requirement for an available 3′ OH, not simply to memorized arrows.
Fill in the concept: New nucleotides are added to the of the growing strand.

4. Antiparallel DNA and complementary base pairing

DNA strands run in opposite directions: one is 5′→3′ and the other is 3′→5′. Complementary bases pair through hydrogen bonds: A–T = 2 and G–C = 3.

Hydrogen-bond calculation: For 5′–ACAGTG–3′ paired with its complement, how many hydrogen bonds form?

5. Chargaff’s rules

In double-stranded DNA, %A = %T, %G = %C, and all four bases total 100%.

Worked example: If C = 18%, then G = 18%. G + C = 36%, leaving 64% for A + T, so A = 32% and T = 32%.
A DNA molecule contains 1,000 nucleotides, including 200 G. How many A nucleotides?

8. Amino-acid structure and R groups

An amino acid has a central α-carbon bonded to an amino group, carboxyl group, H atom, and variable R group. At about cellular pH, amino and carboxyl groups are commonly ionized.

R-group chemistry predicts folding tendencies

Hydrophobic / nonpolarOften clusters away from water in the protein interior.
Hydrophilic / polarOften interacts with water or other polar groups; can form hydrogen bonds.
Ionic / chargedCan form electrostatic attractions with oppositely charged R groups.

9. Peptide bonds and N→C directionality

A peptide bond forms by dehydration/condensation between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another. An –OH and –H are removed as H₂O, and a covalent C–N bond forms.

Directionality: polypeptides have an N-terminus and a C-terminus; sequence is conventionally written N→C.

Peptide vs polypeptide vs protein

TermMeaning

PolypeptideLonger chain with a specific N→C sequence

10. Four levels of protein structure

LevelDescriptionMain stabilizing interactions
PrimaryExact amino-acid sequencePeptide bonds
SecondaryLocal α-helices and β-sheetsHydrogen bonds between backbone groups
TertiaryOverall 3D shape of one polypeptideR-group interactions: hydrophobic, H-bond, ionic, disulfide
QuaternaryAssociation of ≥2 polypeptide subunitsInteractions between subunits
Common misconception: α-helices and β-sheets are stabilized mainly by backbone hydrogen bonds, not by side-chain interactions. Side-chain chemistry is especially important for tertiary structure.
AP connection: A mutation changes primary structure first. Whether function changes depends on how that substitution affects folding, active/binding sites, stability, or subunit interactions.
Need a Hint? — How to reason from mutation → protein function

Ask three questions: (1) Did the amino-acid R-group chemistry change? (2) Is the altered residue near a region important for folding or binding? (3) Would that change affect the final 3D shape or interactions?
